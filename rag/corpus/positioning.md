# Positioning, Stacking, and Switching

## Core Positioning Principle: Control the Kitchen Line

The team that controls the kitchen line wins the majority of pickleball points. At the
kitchen line, you have the angle advantage, the ability to volley, and the ability to attack
pop-ups. At the baseline, you are reactive, can only volley returns with good pace, and have
far less court coverage.

**Default position at the kitchen line:** Both players should stand about 1 inch behind the
NVZ line (to avoid foot faults on volleys). Side-to-side, each player covers roughly half
the court — left player takes the left half, right player takes the right half, with both
covering the middle seam by default.

**Middle priority:** Balls hit toward the middle of the court (especially low, hard drives)
should be taken by the player with their **forehand** in the middle. In a right-left pair
where both are right-handed, the left-side player has their forehand in the middle, so they
take middle balls.

## Stacking

Stacking is a positioning strategy where both players line up on the same side of the court
before the serve or return, then switch to their desired positions after the ball is hit.

**Why stack?** In a right-handed/left-handed pair, you want the left-handed player on the
left side so both players have their forehands in the middle. In a right/right pair, you
might stack so the stronger player takes the forehand side consistently.

**Stacking on serve:**
1. Server stands near the center line (can serve from there)
2. Non-serving partner stands near the sideline, out of bounds, on the same side as the server
3. After the serve, non-serving partner crosses to their preferred side while server moves
   to cover the other half

**Stacking on return:**
1. Returner stands near the baseline on their normal side
2. Non-returning partner stands near the sideline, also out of bounds
3. After the return, both move to their preferred sides before reaching the kitchen

**Semi-stacking:** Only one shot is stacked — usually just the return of serve. Simpler and
sufficient in most recreational play.

## Switching Mid-Rally

Switching (also called "crossing") happens when one player commits to a wide shot and the
other crosses behind them to cover the vacated side.

**When to switch:**
- Your partner hits a wide cross-court shot and physically cannot recover their side
- You are closer to the center and can cover their vacated side faster
- A poach is planned (pre-communicated)

**Communication:** Calling "switch" or "yours" as the ball is hit prevents collisions.
Many unforced errors come from both players going for the same ball or both hesitating.

**When NOT to switch:** If your partner can recover their position with 1-2 steps, switching
creates unnecessary movement and opens the middle seam. Switch only when the positional
advantage is clear.

## Erne

The Erne is an advanced kitchen-line shot where a player jumps from outside the court over
the NVZ corner to volley a ball that would otherwise land in the kitchen. Because you are
airborne - not standing in the kitchen - you can legally volley there.

**Setup:** Works best when the opponent hits a tight down-the-line dink heading toward your
sideline. Step (or run) around the NVZ corner, jump, and volley before landing out of bounds.

**After an Erne:** You have just vacated your side. Your partner must briefly cover the
whole court, or you must re-enter quickly. Pre-communicate with your partner.

## The Middle Seam

"When in doubt, hit the middle" is valid pickleball advice. Balls hit directly between
two opponents create communication confusion - both may go for it, or neither may. The
middle also has the lowest net height (approximately 34 inches vs 36 at the sidelines).

Middle shots are especially effective against teams who:
- Don't have a clear ball-priority rule
- Have a left-handed/right-handed pair who haven't decided who takes middle

## Baseline Positioning (Defense)

When pushed back to the baseline - by a lob, a well-placed smash, or a transition failure -
return to roughly the baseline, slightly inside (about 1-2 feet in). Do not back up behind
the baseline unless tracking a high lob.

**Split-step:** As your opponent contacts the ball, hop into a slight split-step. Feet are
shoulder-width, weight on the balls of your feet. This lets you push off in either direction
quickly. Players who don't split-step are always half a beat late.

## Lob Defense and Recovery

A well-placed lob over the non-dominant shoulder (usually the backhand overhead side) is
the hardest shot in pickleball to defend. When lobbed:

1. Call it: "Back!" so your partner knows you are retreating
2. Turn and run - don't backpedal, you will not catch a good lob backpedaling
3. If you can get behind it, hit a drive or a drop to transition back to the kitchen
4. If you can only hit an overhead while running backward, aim deep cross-court to buy time

Your partner, when one player goes back on a lob, should drop to the transition zone or
baseline to maintain coverage. One player back and one at the kitchen is a very vulnerable
formation - recover together.

## Shot Selection by Court Zone

| Zone | Your Position | Ball Height | Recommended Shot |
|------|---------------|-------------|-----------------|
| Kitchen line | At the NVZ | Below net | Dink cross-court |
| Kitchen line | At the NVZ | Above net | Speed-up or roll |
| Transition | Service line area | Below waist | Drop to kitchen |
| Transition | Service line area | Above waist | Drive or volley |
| Baseline | Back court | Any height | Deep drive or lob |
| Baseline | Back court | High bounce | Overhead drive |
